# Chora: A Reader's Living Memory of the Text

Chora begins with a simple idea: interpretation belongs to the reader. Reading
is an encounter between a person and a text, not the extraction of an answer.
The application should help that encounter accumulate, return, and deepen.

Its guiding question is:

> **Does this deepen the conversation between the reader and the text?**

## The Reader and the Whole

A word, phrase, scene, or construction does not signify in isolation. Its force
can emerge through its relation to a passage, a movement of the argument, other
uses, and the whole dialogue. The whole becomes visible through particulars,
while those particulars become more powerful as their place in the whole
emerges.

Chora therefore preserves both scales:

- the exact Greek word, phrase, or passage that drew attention;
- the larger movement in which it appears;
- other exact locations the reader chooses to bring beside it;
- the reader's changing account of why those locations matter.

Different readers will notice different relationships. Chora must not turn
available material into a verdict about what a reader should see.

## Writing Is the Primary Workspace

The reader can begin writing without first creating a container from a text
selection. A **Vision** is a sustained document that may grow across distant
sections of a work.

The reader can:

- write continuously beside the dialogue;
- bring an exact word, phrase, or passage into the writing;
- continue a thought across several sections;
- return from the writing to every cited location;
- open linguistic material for a Greek word inside the writing.

The selected text supports the thought. It does not contain or limit it.

## Exact Anchors

Every textual placement remains connected to the canonical source rather than
becoming an untraceable quotation.

An anchor preserves:

- the work and canonical text version;
- exact start and end locations;
- the selected Greek;
- its kind, such as word, phrase, or passage;
- enough information to validate and reconstruct the selection.

Anchor identity is deterministic. The same source anchor may appear in several
Visions, and a Vision may place the same anchor more than once. Anchors remain
independent of their visual placements.

An accelerator or reverse index may make discovery faster, but it is never the
authority. If it is incomplete, Chora must use the authoritative anchors and
Vision documents rather than hide valid reader work.

## Relationships Live in the Reader's Prose

Chora does not ask the reader to maintain a technical graph. Relationships grow
through ordinary acts:

1. The reader writes.
2. The reader places exact Greek inside that writing.
3. Chora preserves the source relationship.
4. The reader later encounters something that may matter beside it.
5. Chora can return the reader to the earlier Vision.
6. The reader continues, questions, revises, or leaves the relationship open.

The reader's prose is authoritative. A future graph may be rebuilt from
reader-authored writing and exact placements, but it must not replace that
writing or establish a connection automatically.

Uncertainty is valuable. A question, tension, contrast, echo, or possibility
does not need to be resolved before it can be preserved.

## Word Lookup as Reader Memory

Word Lookup answers two different questions:

1. What linguistic material is available for this occurrence and its possible
   lemmas?
2. Where has this exact occurrence participated in my own writing?

These must remain distinct.

Linguistic material includes candidate lemmas, dictionary entries, morphology,
occurrences, related lexical material, and personal-name research. It may be
incomplete or ambiguous. Chora does not select a contextual meaning from it.

Reader memory comes from exact Vision placements. A phrase or passage placement
also contributes each exact word occurrence it contains, while still opening
the parent placement inside the Vision.

## Reader-Created Structure

Readers may eventually place named markers at exact text locations where they
notice a transition, movement, change in speaker, scene, argument, image, or
mode of inquiry.

Such markers would be personal navigation overlays:

- stable and exact;
- visible without changing the canonical Greek;
- independent of Visions and anchors;
- available as a personal table of contents.

The application should not impose a universal division of the dialogue.

## The Role of a Model

A model is not required for the core reading and writing loop. Exact anchors,
canonical texts, and linguistic material are enough to support reliable return.

A future local model may help retrieve the reader's own earlier writing. It may
return a small set of candidates and explain why each appeared. It must not:

- create or rewrite reader prose;
- make a relationship permanent;
- resolve uncertainty;
- present semantic similarity as linguistic proof;
- decide which dictionary sense governs a passage.

Reader writing remains local unless a future explicit action states exactly
what material will be read and why.

## Product Principles

1. Keep the reader and the text at the center.
2. Preserve exact Greek and exact navigation.
3. Let writing grow across passages without fragmenting it into containers.
4. Keep textual facts, linguistic material, reader writing, and model
   suggestions visibly distinct.
5. Preserve ambiguity and uncertainty.
6. Make relationships discoverable in both directions.
7. Let the reader decide what matters.
8. Keep reader-authored material durable, local, and inspectable.

Chora succeeds when the text remains present, the reader's earlier attention
can return at the right moment, and the application never mistakes assistance
for interpretation.
